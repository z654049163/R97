International Letter Exchanges
Learning Objectives 
Background Information 
Prior to the opening of a conference, a lot of work needs to be done: forming various committees, making contact with experts and scholars, booking venues, etc. The preparation involves enormous letter exchange or correspondences. Learning to write letters according to English conventions is an essential skill for graduate students. In this unit, students will get to know the most common letter formats: the full block format and semi-block format. They will learn how to write an invitation letter, accept an invitation or decline an invitation. As the Internet is spreading out all over the world with increasing accessibility, emails have largely taken place of traditional mails because of its ease and speed. In Task 3, students will also learn to write effective emails for academic purposes.  
Illustration 
Introduction 
The teacher needs to bring the students attention to the format of letters and remind students that the English letters are different from Chinese letters in many aspects. Generally speaking, a letter is composed of SEVEN parts. Highlight the seven parts to help students memorize the format. 
Sample Demonstration 
Three samples are provided for students to get familiar with the format and content of invitation letters. The teacher can guide students to read Sample 1 twice. First, have students focus on the format of the letter, asking them to identify the seven parts of the letter. During the second reading, students can concentrate on the content to see how the invitation is unfolded (see PowerPoint). Sample 3 is a semi-block format with indented paragraphs. Have students compare and contrast the full block format and semi-block format. 
Additional samples are available on Page 4. 
Learning Aids 
 eq \o\ac(
 introducing the full block format and semi-block format. 
 eq \o\ac(
 explaining the content layout of an invitation letter, which can be incorporated into sample demonstration. 
 eq \o\ac(
 providing additional tips for writing invitation letters.
Useful Expressions and Sentence Patterns  
Students should be familiar with these expressions and patterns and learn to use
them when writing invitation letters. The teacher can select to explain some of the sentences that are not covered in the samples. 
Exercises 
The teacher can guide students to finish Exercise 1, 3, and 4 to reinforce the knowledge and skills of letter writing, and use Exercise 2 as a formative assessment to evaluate students
 learning.  
Pedagogical Suggestions 
Choose one sample and guide the students through reading to illustrate the format. Divide students into groups to look at the remaining samples. 
Use Check Your Understanding to check if students have comprehended each sample.

Introduction 
A brief introduction of letters of acceptance and refusal is provided to help students build background knowledge for further learning. 
Sample Demonstration 
Three samples are provided for students to get familiar with the format and content of letters of acceptance and refusal. Instruction should focus on the body of the letter: how to accept or refuse an invitation politely and conventionally (see PowerPoint). Encourage students to explore and generalize the rules for writing letters of acceptance or refusal. Acceptance and refusal letters are supposed to be short and succinct. Refrain from talking about anything irrelevant. 
Learning Aids 
Tips are available for teachers and students and would be better used with specific examples at the teacher
s discretion. In summary, be brief, polite, and unambiguous in writing these letters.  
Useful Expressions and Sentence Patterns  
Students should be familiar with these expressions and patterns and learn to use
them when writing letters. The teacher can select to explain some of the sentences that are not covered in the samples. 
Exercises 
Students use these exercises to practice writing letters of acceptance and refusal. 
Pedagogical Suggestions 
Letters of acceptance and refusal are short and therefore it is much easier for students to create one by modeling the samples. Thus, it is important to expose students to a variety of sentence patterns and expressions to help them learn to vary their sentence patterns. The teacher needs to draw their attention to Useful Expressions and Sentence Patterns and encourage students to add sentence variety in writing practice. 
This task provides a perfect opportunity for peer writing or interactive writing. Students can work in pairs: one writing an invitation letter to invite his/her partner to a conference; the other writing to accept or decline the invitation. When the writing is finished, the teacher can have students read their work to each other and peer-review their letters.

Introduction 
Writing effective emails is considered an essential skill in this electronic age. Students compose and check emails every day but most of them do that in Chinese. They are not aware of the difference between writing an English email and a Chinese one. Emails are practically letters in electronic form. In other words, emails are letters, so they should follow letter writing conventions including the format and the content. 
Sample Demonstration 
Most students have the background knowledge of composing an email. For instance, typing in the recipient
s email address and filling a subject in the subject line. Tell students that if they see Re: appear in the subject line (which usually automatically shows up when you click reply), it means 
concerning
 in Latin, not 
reply.
 When looking at the samples, students need to pay attention to both the format and the content. 
Learning Aids 
This section provides useful tips for writing emails. The teacher can have students read this section by themselves and use some questions to guide their reading and check if they understand these tips. 
Pedagogical Suggestions 
Choose one sample for demonstration to explain the format. 
Have students email each other to practice writing.
